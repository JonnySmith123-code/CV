How to Treat a Pork Pie: A Doctor's Guide

2026-03-03

“I am a proud Lincolnshire pork pie.”

If I told my GP this today, I imagine they would respond with a polite but measured dose of scepticism. They would probe how I came to such an absurd conclusion about my state of being and, crucially, avoid writing “is a Melton Mowbray pie” into my medical notes.

Medicine is used to interpreting the symptoms patients present with. It is, however, less used to interpreting what patients know or think they know about their own biology.

Through the rise of 24/7 health monitoring technologies, direct-to-consumer (D2C) genetic testing, and large language models, we have entered a world in which accessing and exploring personal medical data has never been easier. However, it appears that we have democratised access to complex biomedical information without providing a genuine education on what the data means. As a result, patients now arrive in primary care not just with symptoms, but with reams of data and pre-formed narratives about what it all means for their future health despite most of the population possessing no formal education in science beyond GCSEs.

The narrative I began with is not entirely imagined. In 1944, the Dutch Hunger Winter saw calorie intake in parts of the Netherlands fall to as low as 400 calories per day, with many forced into eating tulip bulbs as a main food source. Decades later, researchers found that the effects of that famine were still detectable – not only in those who lived through it, but in their children and even their grandchildren. Early-life conditions had left lasting marks on how genes were regulated (turned on or off), with measurable health consequences decades later.

The implication is somewhat unsettling. The truth is that “you are what you eat” is only part of the story – it may also be the case that you are, in part, what your grandparents ate. My grandfather, a lifelong enthusiast of the pork pie, may have shaped my own biological risks in ways neither of us fully understands. It is precisely this kind of finding – counterintuitive and easily misinterpreted – that the modern patient is now expected to make sense of.

Clearly this doesn't make me a pork pie, but while obvious, the reasons why are complicated, and this absurd scenario is a caricature of the serious point I am making. As a scientist, I know that our understanding of human biology is nuanced, dynamic, and easy for patients without formal training to mistranslate. Yet consumer health products have opened a treasure trove of data to the public that has the potential to both engage a wider audience with their health, and at the same time cause unwarranted distress or confusion.

Last year, the Labour Government released its 10 Year Health Plan for the NHS, outlining their vision for UK public healthcare in 2035 – a distributed system equipped to detect disease early and prevent where possible before patients reach hospital beds. Integrating digital health tools, wearable devices, and genetic testing into primary care is not just part of that plan – it's effectively the whole plan.

As the NHS undergoes a much-needed restructuring, aiming to incorporate many new advances in the health tech field, I wanted to explore the realities of a health system that increasingly expects patients to arrive in primary care armed with genomic data, wearable metrics, and AI-generated interpretations of their biology. For the first time in decades, UK medicine is being asked to do something new – not just diagnose and treat disease, but rapidly interpret and communicate complex, patient-generated data to those same patients. After nearly three years of working in this field, I wanted to explore what a primary care system already under significant pressure would look like in a new era for the NHS.

The Rise of Patient-Generated Health Data

I'll start by exploring developments in the health tech space.

Interrogating and monitoring the health of ourselves and our loved ones in the absence of a doctor is not a new phenomenon. We have used tools for this purpose long before the Human Genome Project or modern wearables. During the 18th century, in a period when balancing bodily fluids was still considered good medical practice, literate families relied on home health manuals for care, as access to a doctor was slow and often complicated by travel (some things, it seems, have not changed).

The tools of today, however, are clearly distinct in their nature. We have made huge leaps in both the depth and breadth of our understanding of biology, leading to an explosion of consumer-oriented health products over the past two decades. Genetic testing services such as 23andMe, 24/7 wearable devices like Apple Watches and WHOOP, regular blood biomarker analyses, and even longitudinal full-body MRI scans are now accessible to individuals outside traditional clinical settings.

As these technologies become more advanced, their ability to generate sustained behavioural changes is increasingly obvious, for example the impacts of drinking alcohol on wearable metrics have contributed to many changing their attitudes towards alcohol. It seems that the clinical potential of this tech is limited less by its ability to generate health insights, and more by how that data is interpreted by users. Like the medical manuals of the eighteenth century, the outputs of today's tech require careful contextualisation if it's to be clinically useful.

In some technologies, the gap between what customers receive today and what is required for clinical decision-making is substantial. Many D2C genetic tests are not held to the same quality control standards as clinical-grade equivalents, and as such are known to have significantly higher false-positive rates. Furthermore, many tests provide payers with only raw risk percentages without the necessary clinical, environmental, or familial context, frequently leading to an overestimation of disease risk that can be unnecessarily harmful. These are the narratives patients may arrive in consulting rooms with.

Crucially, of the millions who have already done a genetic test, many don't appreciate that the clinical interpretation of their DNA is liable to change. If you were to take a test today and analyse the results again in five years' time, there's a realistic chance that the associated risk predictions would be different. Though by how much is impossible to say, these changes are difficult for patients to understand, particularly when they're initially led to believe they're at increased risk of a serious condition, only to later be told that they're not.

A person who is considered a pork pie by science one day may be considered a Shepherd's pie years later – not because anything intrinsic to them has changed, but because our understanding of biology has.

In an ideal world, all individuals taking genetic tests would have access to genetic counselling to discuss the implications and uncertainties of their results in addition to being updated when our understanding of their unique risks changes. In practice though, this is rarely possible within our system which has only 300 trained genetic counsellors. And so, for many, interpretation is fragmented or missing altogether – leaving the role of translating complex science to no one in particular. While private providers such as Bupa are beginning to offer such services, access remains uneven, and a large proportion of individuals are left to make sense of their results alone – often turning to online tools that act as today's equivalent of studying early medical manuals.

This void is increasingly being filled by LLMs. Data published by OpenAI suggests that around 5% of all ChatGPT prompts are health-related, meaning users collectively ask over 230 million health queries each week. Major LLM players have responded by launching dedicated health models co-developed with physicians. This field is developing rapidly, and it will be interesting to see how healthcare systems and regulators interact with these tools and their developers.

This issue of extra information leading to increased uncertainty is not isolated to genetic tests; as I found during my own sports career, wearables often produce uncontextualised alarms. I regularly unnerved both myself and my coaches by recording a maximum heart rate of 220 bpm on my Garmin – well above what would typically be expected. On its own, this data suggested a potential problem that could affect my ability to compete. It was only after combining long-term monitoring with ultrasound imaging and a clinical assessment that I was reassured this reflected normal variation rather than pathology.

These technologies embody both the limitations and the potential of a modern-day health system, one increasingly reliant on the individual to detect abnormalities in lieu of a physician. In isolation, data can mislead and cause concern. Integrated over time, and interpreted with adequate context, it can become genuinely useful. Had I not had my Garmin and there was a serious issue, I would probably have missed it entirely. It was only through further investigation that I was able to fully understand whether I was safe to train or not. This is a great example of preventative care.

The NHS Vision and the Reality of Delivery

This story also emphasises the vision outlined in the 10 Year Plan – a technology-led system in which diverse data streams collected by both patients and doctors are combined throughout a lifetime to enable personalised, preventative care at scale in the community rather than the hospital.

This vision represents a long-sought-after shift from reactionary care for the sick to anticipatory care for the many. Translating that vision into practice, however, will not be straightforward but it's essential. After many years of financial squeezes and amid growing fears surrounding our ageing population, the NHS looks like it's on its last legs – as the Plan's authors put it, “The choice for the NHS is stark: reform or die.”

As I have already outlined, the technologies underpinning this new NHS have already been developed and millions of Brits have already had some interaction with them – whether it be an Apple Watch or Ancestry.com test. The use of this tech is not the biggest change proposed in the plan – the revolution lies in a necessary rebalancing act that asks individuals to take more responsibility for their own health and ease the burden on our strained secondary care systems. As Professor Andrew Scott, the Senior Director of Economics at EIT, has explained, improving long-term health outcomes depends on sustained engagement with prevention across a patient's lifetime, rather than intermittent intervention once disease has developed.

In practice, this means patients are expected to engage more actively with their own health data outside of traditional clinical settings – as the plan puts it, the NHS intends to “put power in patients' hands.” In turn, the NHS will build systems to interpret and integrate this data into care pathways.

A prevention-based NHS then will rely heavily on individual behaviour, something that decades of public health efforts have shown is incredibly difficult to nudge sustainably. The huge revenues of cardiometabolic drugs for obesity and type II diabetes in many ways reflect both the scale of this challenge and the failure of previous efforts to generate behavioural change through policy.

The global obesity drug market is anticipated to reach $150 billion by 2035, driven by sales of GLP-1 receptor agonists such as Ozempic.

In this context, the Plan's ambition to build a more distributed model of healthcare in which responsibility is shared more explicitly between individuals and the system raises an important question: how can we support patients to generate and act on complex health information in a consistent and meaningful way?

Ensuring access to these technologies is perhaps the most important step to ensuring the data is generated. The Plan promises to “make wearables standard in preventative, chronic and post-acute NHS treatment by 2035,” as well as committing to free devices in high-need areas. They also propose a population-wide genetic risk screening programme and universal genome sequencing for newborns. Assuming these efforts are successfully implemented (a big assumption), this would ensure that every patient arriving in primary care would have some form of predictive biological data on record.

The GP of the Future

The question, then, is not whether this data will arrive, it's whether the GP on the receiving end will be equipped to deal with it. Some GPs may argue that assessing a patient's risk for major diseases through family history, BMI, and lifestyle habits alone is enough, and that the first step patients can take to protect their health would be to make better lifestyle choices. While useful indicators of future morbidity, in an era of prevention for the masses, we must find new ways to help healthy patients stay healthy for longer – in alignment with the Plan's ethos of proactive care – as well as continuing to target unhealthy behaviours.

If we're able to build the data infrastructure, we'll also need to change the way current and future GPs think and act. A GP ‘fit for the future’ must be able to efficiently deal with a patient who arrives with a genetic test suggesting elevated risk for cardiovascular disease, a smartwatch flagging irregular heart rhythms, and a ChatGPT-generated summary of what it might all mean. They must decide whether data should be trusted, thought of differently, or altogether ignored. They must then communicate this judgement to the patient who is engaging with their health so they can understand what actions should be taken to maximise their health.

Unlike many government plans, there is room for optimism in this vision for the NHS. The UK has already championed some of the world's most comprehensive public health datasets in UK Biobank and Genomics England, and the plan commits an extra £600 million in public and private funds to the building of a new Health Data Research Service.

It's worth noting that Mark Zuckerberg alone has committed $10 billion to health data research over the next decade – nearly twenty times what this plan promises over the same period.

However, one of the great limitations of today's tech revolution is that our ability to generate vast quantities of data has improved significantly while our ability to develop systems capable of harnessing it effectively has not kept pace. Proving that we can overcome this hurdle will be a decisive step towards revitalising an ashen NHS.

It's essential that we make an equivalent investment in the workforce needed to interpret patient data in everyday clinical care, details of which are less well defined in the Plan. The government outlines funding for a new data-rich NHS without fully explaining how this data will be interpreted and by whom. Are GPs expected to also become quasi-genetic counsellors or are we to rely on AI chatbots? It's not clear yet.

I recently attended a lecture by a former GP, now Professor of Primary Care at the University of Oxford, who argued that no amount of technology and data will replace the role of the GP. I must agree. If anything, the rise of patient-generated data makes that role even more important, not less. It's noteworthy that since this current government came into power, there are nearly 1,500 more FTE GPs with plans to train thousands more, something that is desperately needed if their vision is to be realised.

The human component required to deploy a system so technologically rich would be easy to underestimate, but to do so would be ill-advised. Primary care teams will be at the heart of this new NHS, and they must effectively adapt to their new roles as data translators and risk interpreters en masse if patients are to take more agency over their own health. The combination of effective technology and training will be crucial to building this new era of UK healthcare.

I am not a pork pie. But the GP who once greeted that remark with polite scepticism must, in the surgery of the future, greet it with curiosity instead. Simply correcting a patient's misunderstanding of their data before moving on will not be enough. GPs must sit with patients in their uncertainty, revisit it, and help them make sense of a biological tale that is still being written. Only then can patients bear the weight of an NHS that will increasingly lean on them for support.
